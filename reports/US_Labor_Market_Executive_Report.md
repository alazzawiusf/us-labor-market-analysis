# U.S. Labor Market Intelligence

## An Executive Analysis of Geographic Growth, Industry Transformation, and Occupational Opportunity

**Analytical period:** QCEW state employment and pay, 2015–2025 · QCEW industry growth, principally 2015–2024 · OEWS occupational structure, 2025 (underlying data coverage, 2021–2025)  
**Prepared by:** Ahmed Alazzawi  
**Sources:** U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics (BLS), Quarterly Census of Employment and Wages (QCEW) and Occupational Employment and Wage Statistics (OEWS)  
**Report date:** October 2026  
**Explore the full data:** [Jump to the two interactive dashboards](#interactive-dashboards-explore-the-full-analysis) · [Project methodology and code](https://github.com/alazzawiusf/us-labor-market-analysis)

> **Central assessment:** U.S. labor-market performance is geographically uneven, industry-driven, and occupationally specialized. Employment expansion, compensation growth, and concentration of skilled work do not move together in a single, uniform pattern. Decisions based on a state's headline job-growth rate alone risk overlooking the industries producing that growth, the path taken to achieve it, and the types of occupations concentrated in its workforce.

---

## Executive Summary

The principal finding of this analysis is not merely that some states grew faster than others. It is that **state employment growth, wage growth, industrial composition, and occupational specialization represent different dimensions of labor-market performance**. Bringing them together provides a more discriminating view of regional opportunity and exposure than any one measure in isolation.

### Findings that matter

| Evidence | Observed result | Executive interpretation |
|---|---:|---|
| Median state employment change, 2015–2025 | **+8.44%** | State experiences diverged widely around the middle of the distribution. |
| Highest measured state employment growth | **Idaho +31.51%** | Fast expansion was concentrated in a subset of states, particularly in the West and South. |
| Employment-growth / nominal-pay-growth correlation across 51 state/DC jurisdictions | **r = 0.602** | Hiring expansion and average pay generally moved together, but neither substitutes for the other. |
| Health Care and Social Assistance among top three industry job-gain contributors, 2015–2024 | **47 of 50 covered jurisdictions** | Health care was a pervasive employment-growth contributor, not a story limited to a few states. |
| Health Care and Social Assistance ranked first for industry job gains | **36 of 50 covered jurisdictions** | Health care led in most covered jurisdictions, while the other growth engines differed locally. |
| Washington, 2025 Computer and Mathematical occupation concentration | **LQ = 2.00** | Occupational specialization can identify a region's distinctive employment structure beyond job totals. |

Three implications follow. **First**, strong endpoint growth should be assessed alongside annual trajectories: rapid recovery after a shock is not equivalent to uninterrupted expansion. **Second**, the industrial composition of hiring matters: a state can grow substantially even while a historically important industry contracts. **Third**, apparent occupational opportunity should be evaluated using employment scale, pay, and geographic concentration together; each answers a different question.

This report is a descriptive, comparative assessment, **not a forecast or a causal model**. It offers a disciplined basis for investigating talent markets, economic-development priorities, and location decisions. It does not estimate labor shortages, future vacancies, hiring costs, or the causal impact of industrial change.

## 1. Geographic Divergence: Where Employment Expanded—and Where It Did Not

### 1.1 Growth was distributed unevenly

QCEW official state-total data show a pronounced dispersion in employment growth from 2015 to 2025. Across the 50 states and the District of Columbia, the **median** employment increase was **8.44%**, the **mean** was **9.74%**, and state-level outcomes ranged from **−1.72% to +31.51%**. The mean exceeding the median is consistent with a set of high-growth states lifting the overall average; these figures do not, on their own, establish the shape of the entire distribution.

| Fastest-growing jurisdictions | Employment change, 2015–2025 | Weakest outcomes | Employment change, 2015–2025 |
|---|---:|---|---:|
| Idaho | +31.51% | North Dakota | −1.72% |
| Utah | +29.87% | District of Columbia | −0.26% |
| Nevada | +26.08% | Wyoming | −0.15% |
| Arizona | +23.95% | Louisiana | −0.14% |
| Florida | +23.32% | West Virginia | −0.11% |
| Texas | +21.05% | — | — |

The first six states exceeded the median by a wide margin. Several of the weakest outcomes were near zero rather than showing large absolute contractions, an important distinction when describing underperformance. The geographic map helps reveal the clustering and discontinuities that a rank-ordered table cannot convey as efficiently.

### 1.2 The same endpoint can hide different histories

The period contains a major labor-market discontinuity in 2020. Consequently, interpreting 2015–2025 growth as though every state advanced along a smooth path would be misleading.

**Idaho and Utah** combined strong pre-2020 expansion with comparatively limited pandemic-era employment declines and relatively rapid subsequent recovery. **Florida** also had strong momentum before 2020, but its employment fell **4.9% in 2020** before a substantial rebound during 2021–2023. These distinctions matter because a 2025 index above its 2015 level can reflect either sustained addition of jobs or the recovery of previously lost employment, alongside later expansion.

The contrasting cases are instructive. **Hawaii's employment contracted approximately 15.0% in 2020**, making it inappropriate to portray its long-term outcome simply as a decade of persistent stagnation. **North Dakota's employment fell 4.6% in 2016**, well before the pandemic, and experienced another decline in 2020; its weak endpoint thus reflects a materially different sequence of events.

**Decision significance.** A location assessment should test *how* growth occurred, not simply whether it occurred. Useful follow-up measures include continuity of expansion, size of cyclical contractions, time to recovery, and exposure to particular industries. This analysis documents the differences in trajectories but does not calculate a formal resilience index or forecast subsequent performance.

## 2. Employment Growth and Compensation: Related, Not Interchangeable

### 2.1 A positive relationship with important exceptions

Across the 51 state/DC jurisdictions, the correlation between **2015–2025 employment growth** and **nominal average annual pay growth** was approximately **0.602**. States growing faster in employment generally also tended to post larger increases in reported average pay. This is a meaningful descriptive relationship, but it does **not** show that employment expansion caused wages to rise—or that every fast-growing state experienced above-median pay growth.

The median nominal average-pay increase across jurisdictions was **47.16%**, versus the **8.44%** median for employment growth. Dividing jurisdictions at these two sample medians produces four useful *exploratory* profiles:

| Employment growth / nominal pay growth | Jurisdictions | Illustrative examples |
|---|---:|---|
| Above median / above median | 21 | Idaho, Utah, Nevada, Arizona, Florida |
| Above median / below median | 5 | Texas, Alabama, Delaware, New Jersey, Kentucky |
| Below median / above median | 5 | New Hampshire, Nebraska, Massachusetts, Hawaii, Vermont |
| Below median / below median | 20 | Remaining jurisdictions in the lower/lower group |

These are **relative classifications within this sample**, not formal economic thresholds for a strong or weak labor market. Texas, for example, recorded **+21.05%** employment growth while falling below the sample median on *nominal pay growth*. That does not mean Texas wages were low in absolute terms; it means pay *growth* was lower relative to the other jurisdictions in this comparison.

### 2.2 Nominal pay growth requires careful interpretation

Average annual pay increased in nominal terms across every jurisdiction in the analyzed period. The mean increase was **47.61%**, with a range from **33.30% to 75.91%**. Washington recorded the highest growth (**+75.91%**), followed by Idaho (**+60.57%**), Maine (**+57.42%**), Florida (**+57.31%**), and California (**+57.16%**).

These increases do **not** establish equivalent gains in living standards. The analysis does not adjust for inflation, regional price levels, or cost of living. Nor is QCEW *average annual pay* equivalent to the pay raise received by a typical continuing employee. It is an aggregate measure that can move because of changes in the composition of employment, compensation patterns, and the industries represented in the state.

The pandemic period makes this qualification especially important:

| State | 2020 employment change | 2020 average-pay change |
|---|---:|---:|
| Florida | −4.9% | +8.0% |
| Hawaii | −15.0% | +10.0% |
| Washington | −5.3% | +10.3% |

Employment contracted at the same time that average pay rose in each case. **One possible explanation** is a workforce-composition effect—for instance, a disproportionate loss of lower-paid jobs can mechanically raise the remaining average. These data do not isolate that mechanism or prove it was the sole driver. They do show why a rising average should not be reported as an equal increase in individual wages.

**Decision significance.** For employers comparing locations, nominal average-pay growth can serve as a signal deserving further compensation analysis, but salary benchmarking requires occupation-specific wage distributions, local price information, and potentially more granular geography. For policymakers, aggregate wage growth should not be mistaken for a universal improvement in worker well-being.

## 3. What Drove Employment Growth? Industry Evidence Changes the Story

### 3.1 One widely shared growth engine; different secondary drivers

The industry analysis compares the change in industry employment within jurisdictions. Because confidentiality suppression reduced industry coverage for some states in 2025, the **systematic cross-state industry ranking uses 2015–2024** and requires **at least 95% industry-to-official-state employment coverage at both endpoints**. Under that rule, **50 of the 51 state/DC jurisdictions** qualified; **Delaware was excluded**. The industry sample should therefore not be read as a complete 51-jurisdiction census.

Within these sufficiently covered jurisdictions, **Health Care and Social Assistance** stood out in both frequency and scale. It ranked among the **top three industries for net job gains in 47 of 50 jurisdictions** and ranked **first in 36**—72% of the covered group. The summed increase across the included states was approximately **4.00 million jobs** for this industry.

| Industry | Number of covered jurisdictions where industry ranked among top 3 for net job gains |
|---|---:|
| Health Care and Social Assistance | **47** |
| Professional, Scientific, and Technical Services | **33** |
| Construction | **24** |
| Transportation and Warehousing | **20** |
| Accommodation and Food Services | **8** |
| Manufacturing | **4** |

*The top-three counts are not mutually exclusive: each jurisdiction can contribute up to three industries to this table.*

Two conclusions emerge. First, health care's role was **geographically widespread**, rather than being explained solely by employment gains in the largest states. Second, beyond health care, the mix of industries underpinning growth was **far from uniform**. Professional and technical services, construction, and transportation/warehousing recur, but in different combinations and rankings.

The distinction is important: a national industry can be a common contributor without every jurisdiction sharing the same overall economic growth model.

### 3.2 Different states can reach strong growth through different industry mixes

Three state case studies illustrate how sector-level composition changes the interpretation of statewide performance. The case-study amounts below are the previously analyzed **2015–2025** changes for those states; unlike the systematic 2015–2024 comparison, these endpoint figures require state/industry suppression and coverage checks before any finer-grained claim.

#### Florida: expansion across several large sectors

| Industry | Net employment change, 2015–2025 |
|---|---:|
| Health Care and Social Assistance | +333,648 |
| Professional, Scientific, and Technical Services | +256,459 |
| Construction | +228,144 |
| Transportation and Warehousing | +178,131 |
| Accommodation and Food Services | +147,126 |

Florida's top five industries accounted for **approximately 61%** of its total state employment gain in the underlying analysis. The picture is one of substantial expansion across multiple large categories rather than growth confined to a single sector. At the same time, a 61% contribution from five industries means that performance should not be described as evenly distributed across the whole economy. A workforce planner would need to examine how demand for particular occupations maps to these sectors before drawing recruitment conclusions.

#### Idaho: construction was unusually prominent

Idaho's significant contributors included **Health Care (+38,691)**, **Construction (+36,954)**, **Accommodation and Food Services (+20,886)**, and **Professional and Technical Services (+19,709)**. Employment in construction increased approximately **102%** over the analyzed period. That percentage is a strong indicator of industry-specific expansion, but its significance should be read alongside Idaho's smaller employment base; a large percentage change is not automatically a larger nationwide addition of jobs than a smaller percentage change in a more populous state.

The broader interpretation is that **two high-growth states need not face the same occupational and industry mix**. Idaho's construction expansion points to a different sectoral balance than a state where professional services dominate net job additions.

#### Texas: overall growth despite an industry contraction

Texas recorded major additions in **Health Care (+392,834)**, **Professional and Technical Services (+354,280)**, **Accommodation and Food Services (+244,890)**, **Construction (+222,918)**, and **Transportation and Warehousing (+217,157)**. Simultaneously, **Mining, Quarrying, and Oil and Gas Extraction declined by 60,136 jobs (−22.28%)**.

This provides an important counterexample to simplified regional narratives. Texas achieved substantial statewide employment expansion **despite** a net contraction in mining/oil/gas employment. It would be misleading to characterize the period's net statewide job growth as being driven by an expanding oil-and-gas workforce. The result instead indicates that employment additions in other industries outweighed losses in that category. It does not establish that oil and gas was economically unimportant: employment change is not the same as output, investment, productivity, or export value.

### 3.3 What the industry results do—and do not—tell us

The top-growth-industry analysis identifies **net changes**, not gross hires, vacancies, employment churn, or the causes of employer demand. Industry rank also depends on a jurisdiction's starting employment distribution. A sector that adds the most jobs may not have the fastest percentage growth, and a fast-growing small industry may not account for a large share of total job additions.

The analytical implication is therefore to assess **industry contribution, percentage expansion, employment base, and coverage quality separately**. That is especially critical when comparing industries affected by disclosure suppression.

## 4. Occupational Structure: Scale, Specialization, and Pay Are Different Signals

The occupational layer adds information that industry employment totals cannot provide. Industries describe **where workers are employed by business activity**; occupations describe **what kinds of work people perform**. The two systems complement each other, but they are not directly interchangeable or a proven one-to-one crosswalk.

The analysis primarily uses the **2025 OEWS occupational cross-section** to compare employment scale, wages, and geographic concentration. Data for 2021–2025 support preparation and exploration, but OEWS multi-panel estimation means those years should not be treated as a simple annual job-growth sequence.

### 4.1 Why concentration matters in addition to headcount

Nationally, some of the largest major occupational groups in 2025 included **Office and Administrative Support (approximately 17.75 million jobs)**, **Food Preparation and Serving (13.68 million)**, **Transportation and Material Moving (13.68 million)**, **Sales (13.42 million)**, and **Management (11.13 million)**.

High employment volume does not necessarily imply a distinctive regional specialization. **Location Quotient (LQ)** compares the share of a state's employment in an occupation with that occupation's share nationally:

**LQ = (occupation employment / total employment in state) ÷ (occupation employment / total employment nationally).**

An LQ above **1.0** signals an occupation is relatively more concentrated in the state than in the nation overall. It does **not** mean there are more openings, higher wages, faster expected growth, or better employment prospects for any particular worker.

Selected 2025 specializations from the analysis illustrate how distinctive state occupational profiles can be:

| Jurisdiction | Major occupational group | Location Quotient |
|---|---|---:|
| Washington | Computer and Mathematical | **2.00** |
| Michigan | Architecture and Engineering | **1.76** |
| Indiana | Production | **2.01** |
| Wyoming | Construction and Extraction | **2.20** |
| California | Farming, Fishing, and Forestry | **4.08** |
| District of Columbia | Legal | **7.08** |

An especially high value, such as the District of Columbia's Legal LQ, identifies **relative employment concentration**, not a direct measurement of the size or growth rate of legal hiring. Indeed, the analysis found that Office and Administrative Support had a comparatively narrow state LQ range (**0.67–1.14**) while Legal ranged from **0.58–7.08**. Large occupational groups can be broadly distributed; smaller groups may more sharply distinguish a local labor market.

Washington's 2025 **Computer and Mathematical** major group had an **LQ of 2.00**, but the detailed occupations behind it differed substantially in size and concentration:

The contrast is analytically useful. **Software Developers** combined a large local employment base with above-national concentration; **Statisticians**, although far fewer in number, were more unusually concentrated relative to their national employment share. **Research Scientists** displayed a high reported mean wage but a small estimated employment base compared with Software Developers.

A single label such as *technology opportunity* therefore obscures important differences in market scale and professional specialization. The numbers support an occupational profile; they do not directly establish vacancies, hiring difficulty, future demand, or how many openings are accessible to a particular candidate.

*Measure note: The Washington table uses **mean annual wages**, as reported in the underlying detailed-occupation findings. The interactive opportunity profile may display **median** wages for its major groups; these measures should not be substituted for one another.*

### 4.3 Specialization and pay do not follow one universal pattern

The exploratory cross-state analysis found sharply different relationships between occupational concentration and the pay measure used in that analysis. Illustrative **2025 LQ/pay correlations** included **Computer and Mathematical (r ≈ +0.845)** and **Business and Financial Operations (r ≈ +0.763)**, versus **Production (r ≈ −0.502)** and **Office and Administrative Support (r ≈ −0.484)**. Healthcare Practitioners and Technical occupations also had a negative association (**r ≈ −0.376**).

These contrasts are important precisely because they **reject a universal proposition** that regional specialization automatically brings higher occupational pay. The direction of association varies by occupational family. Geographic cost structure, industry mix, specialization within broad occupational codes, and other omitted factors could influence these patterns. The coefficients are descriptive and should not be interpreted causally or transferred from one occupational category to another.

## 5. Occupational Wage Dispersion: The Median Is Not the Whole Pay Story

Compensation assessment becomes incomplete when an entire major occupational group is represented by only one median or mean. The OEWS **10th-to-90th percentile wage range**, viewed alongside the median, offers a more informative picture of the reported spread in pay within an occupational group.

This changes the questions an executive or analyst can ask. Two occupational groups could have comparable median wages while exhibiting substantially different lower-tail and upper-tail outcomes. Conversely, a high upper-percentile wage does not mean that the typical worker in that occupation earns that amount. The 10th and 90th percentiles should also not be mistaken for statistical confidence limits, minimum or maximum wages, or starting and senior-level salaries.

## 6. Integrated Assessment: Reading the Labor Market as a System

The combined findings suggest a three-layer interpretation rather than a one-dimensional ranking of states.

### Layer A — Growth and trajectory

State employment change identifies expansion or contraction, while the historical path distinguishes **sustained growth**, **pre-existing weakness**, and **shock/recovery dynamics**. Idaho, Utah, Florida, Hawaii, and North Dakota illustrate why this distinction matters. For decisions sensitive to stability, a growth endpoint should be paired with historical volatility and industry exposure, neither of which is fully summarized by a ten-year percentage change.

### Layer B — Sources of growth

Industry data reveal whether job additions were concentrated in similar sectors or arose from different local combinations. Health Care and Social Assistance was consistently prominent across jurisdictions, but the relative importance of construction, professional services, and transportation varied. The Florida–Idaho–Texas cases show why industrial composition matters even among places with strong statewide employment expansion.

### Layer C — Structure of work and compensation

OEWS adds a further distinction between **large labor markets** and **distinctive occupational concentrations**. Washington's detailed technology occupations illustrate that a state may combine high employment scale in one occupation with even stronger relative concentration in another. Pay adds a third dimension, and occupational wage percentiles reveal dispersion that group-level medians conceal.

### A more defensible decision framework

| Decision question | Primary evidence in this project | What must still be checked before acting |
|---|---|---|
| Which states experienced sustained employment expansion? | QCEW state totals and annual trajectories | Latest conditions, metro-level variation, cyclical exposure |
| Which sectors accounted for net job additions? | QCEW state-by-industry changes and coverage controls | Suppression impact, business-level trends, sector forecasts |
| Does a fast-growing state have unusually strong pay growth? | State employment/pay comparison | Inflation, regional cost of living, occupation-specific wages |
| Where is a specialized occupational workforce concentrated? | OEWS employment and LQ | Skills, supply of available workers, commuting markets, vacancies |
| What compensation ranges exist within occupational groups? | OEWS median and percentile wages | Job level, experience, location, current recruitment data |

A responsible use of the report would be to **screen and prioritize additional investigation**, rather than to declare a state the single best location or an occupation a guaranteed opportunity. Such a ranking would require explicit decision weights, current vacancy data, costs, sector outlooks, and—depending on the application—metro-level analysis that this project does not contain.

## 7. Strategic Implications for Business and Policy

### Workforce and talent strategy

An organization expanding into a new geography should distinguish **labor-market size** from **relative specialization**. A large headcount in an occupational category may indicate a broad existing workforce, while a high LQ signals that the occupation is unusually prevalent in the local mix. Neither alone measures how many qualified workers are looking for jobs. Combine both with observed occupational wages and external vacancy or talent-availability evidence before estimating recruiting feasibility.

### Location and operating footprint analysis

A state with strong long-term employment growth may have undergone very different forms of expansion. States reliant on a small number of growth industries might merit different sensitivity tests than states where job gains are spread across several sectors. The findings support considering **sector concentration and the path of employment growth** alongside growth rate; they do not support a claim that one growth structure is necessarily more resilient without testing that proposition.

### Compensation planning

Aggregate state pay growth can be a useful first indicator of changing compensation conditions, but occupational wage percentiles are more suitable for narrowing compensation questions. The pandemic-era evidence illustrates why average pay can rise while employment falls. Employers should avoid treating QCEW average-pay growth as a direct estimate of raises, or as a forecast of salaries required for future hires.

### Economic development and training priorities

Health care's breadth across state growth profiles suggests it deserves sustained attention in many local workforce discussions. But the diversity of secondary industry drivers cautions against adopting one uniform training or development agenda across jurisdictions. Local occupational profiles can help frame follow-up questions about sector-linked skills, while actual training investments require projections, credential requirements, and employer-demand evidence beyond this descriptive study.

## 8. Data, Definitions, and Analytical Reliability

### 8.1 Data design and periods

This analysis joins insights across **three related but distinct analytical datasets**:

| Analytical layer | BLS series | Principal period | Primary purpose |
|---|---|---|---|
| State performance | QCEW official state totals | 2015–2025 | Employment and nominal average-pay growth |
| Industry dynamics | QCEW state × industry | 2015–2024 for systematic comparison; 2015–2025 state cases where examined | Net industry employment additions and losses |
| Occupational structure | OEWS state × occupation | 2025 cross-section; data prepared for 2021–2025 | Employment, concentration, wage levels and percentiles |

The QCEW state total series is the authoritative source for reported state-level employment in this project. **It was not reconstructed by summing industry rows**. The industry dataset is a separate analytical layer affected by confidentiality suppression.

### 8.2 Suppression and industry coverage

Industry records were assembled from QCEW ownership components. Approximately **32.5%** of state-industry rows carried a suppression indicator, meaning **at least one underlying ownership component** was not disclosed. This does **not** mean 32.5% of all jobs were missing: an affected industry aggregate may be partially observed rather than entirely unavailable. Suppressed values were **not imputed**.

Across the full state-year coverage assessment, the mean ratio of industry employment to the official state employment total was approximately **98.92%** (median **99.82%**). The overall coverage was generally high, but 2025 was a material exception in some places: mean state-level industry coverage fell to **92.89%**, with a minimum of **52.84%**. Examples of low 2025 coverage included Wyoming (**52.84%**), Vermont (**59.71%**), Alaska (**71.39%**), and the District of Columbia (**75.39%**).

To protect the cross-state comparison, the main industry-driver analysis therefore uses 2015–2024 with **≥95% endpoint employment coverage** at both ends. This yielded **50 qualifying state/DC jurisdictions**; using 2015–2025 under the same rule would have yielded only **29**. Rankings from the selected industry comparison are valid **within that coverage-defined sample**, not guaranteed for missing/suppressed components or excluded jurisdictions.

### 8.3 OEWS estimation and missing values

OEWS estimates use a multi-panel survey and estimation methodology; differences between its annual published estimates are **not** used here as a simple direct measure of annual job creation. The occupational sections instead emphasize the 2025 structure, wages, and location quotients.

In the 2025 detailed-occupation preparation, the analytical inventory recorded **35,223 state/occupation rows**, with missing employment and LQ in **1,281 rows (3.64%)** and missing mean/median annual wages in **699 rows (1.98%)**. Missing or suppressed values were retained as missing rather than changed to zero. Occupational comparisons should therefore account for disclosure and availability, especially when interpreting small specialized fields.

### 8.4 Measures and interpretation rules

- **Employment growth (%)** = `(Employment_end / Employment_start − 1) × 100`; a change between endpoints, not necessarily an annualized rate.
- **Net industry employment change** = `Industry employment_end − Industry employment_start`; not gross hiring or vacancies.
- **Nominal average-pay growth (%)** = `(Average annual pay_end / Average annual pay_start − 1) × 100`; not inflation-adjusted and not equal to the typical employee's raise.
- **Location Quotient (LQ)** compares an occupation's share of employment in a state with its share in national employment; LQ above 1 denotes relative concentration.
- **Occupational median wage** is the wage level dividing the reported wage distribution in half; **mean wage** is its arithmetic average. They are not equivalent measures.
- **10th–90th wage percentiles** summarize reported wage dispersion, not forecast bands or statistical confidence intervals.
- **Pearson correlation** summarizes linear association; it does not prove causation and may be affected by omitted variables.

### 8.5 Scope boundaries

This report does not adjust state pay growth for inflation or purchasing power, formally estimate labor-demand elasticities, infer unobserved suppressed industry values, assess migration or productivity as causes of change, or measure current vacancies or unemployment. Occupation and industry categories follow different classifications, so the report does not claim to have traced specific QCEW industry gains directly into specific OEWS occupation changes. National occupational totals and state-level concentration statistics are descriptive estimates, not an employer-level workforce census.

## Conclusion

U.S. labor-market change is best understood not as a single race among states but as the interaction of **geographic trajectories, industrial employment contributions, and occupational structures**.

The evidence is especially strong on four points. **State employment growth was uneven**; a small set of states experienced far faster expansion than the median. **Employment and nominal pay growth were related but not synonymous**, with clear exceptions and potentially important composition effects. **Health Care and Social Assistance was the most widespread measured engine of industry employment gains**, even as construction, professional services, and other sectors shaped distinct local growth patterns. **Occupational specialization added information that neither jobs totals nor headline wages could supply**, and its relationship with pay varied meaningfully across occupational families.

The most decision-relevant takeaway is methodological as well as substantive: **start with the outcome, identify the industries beneath it, examine the occupations and wage structure behind the local workforce, and validate coverage before converting a pattern into a recommendation.** Used in that sequence, the indicators provide a defensible framework for labor-market intelligence—while leaving room for the additional data necessary for an actual investment, site-selection, or talent-acquisition decision.
